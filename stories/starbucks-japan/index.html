A map of Japan · A question of population

Who is the
coffee for?

2,108 Starbucks stores. One deceptively simple question: does dividing by population tell us who they serve?

By Kohei Masuda · April 17, 2026

Revised October 4, 2026 · Store data remains the April 2026 snapshot

Follow the map↓

A map of coffee shops can look like a map of prosperity. Dense clusters suggest busy cities; blank spaces suggest somewhere being left behind. But before reading an economic story into the pattern, we need to ask what the numbers actually count.

Start with 2,108 Starbucks locations across Japan. Divide by the number of residents, and some very small places rise to the top. Four of them reveal the same problem in different ways: the people who live inside a boundary are not everyone who spends time there.

01 / Start with the count

The big places stand out.

2,108 stores across 47 prefectures

Each dot is one store at its recorded location. Dots overlap in the busiest areas. Tokyo has 432, about one in five of the national total. But large populations can support large totals. What happens when we account for the people who live there?

Residents are counted where they live. Stores may also serve people arriving for work, shopping or travel. These ratios do not measure those journeys, but they give us a reason to look more closely at central Tokyo.

05 / Chiyoda, Tokyo

A city beyond its residents.

69.7 stores per 100,000 residents

48 stores / 68,835 residents

Chiyoda ranks first. But this central Tokyo ward also fills with people coming to work or study. The ward’s 2020 census figures show a daytime population far larger than its resident population. A resident-only ratio leaves that daily change outside the calculation.

The census provides context; all store ratios still use the 2025 resident dataset.

06 / Hiezu, Tottori

One café. A whole village ratio.

27.7 stores per 100,000 residents

1 store / 3,616 residents

Hiezu comes second with just one store, in AEON Mall Hiezu. A village boundary gives us a resident count, but it does not define a shopping trip. The mall location suggests a wider customer base; this dataset does not measure where shoppers come from.

07 / Taga, Shiga

A stop along the way.

27.2 stores per 100,000 residents · third nationally

2 stores / 7,346 residents

Both stores in Taga are at the Meishin Expressway’s Taga Service Area: one on each side of the highway. The town’s residents supply the denominator, but the location also serves people stopping on a journey.

A high ratio need not mean cafés lining the town’s streets. Here, two nearby highway stops lift a small town to third place. The store data does not tell us where customers live.

Sources for the two service-area stores. The points are close together at this scale.

08 / Tajiri, Osaka

The airport changes the question.

23.6 stores per 100,000 residents

2 stores / 8,484 residents

Tajiri ranks fourth and includes part of Kansai International Airport. Its two stores in this snapshot are in Terminal 1, where travelers and workers join the potential customers. Dividing by the town's residents describes a location. It does not describe the number of people passing through the terminals.

09 / Inside Fukuoka Prefecture

One color hides many places.

The prefecture’s average is 1.79 stores per 100,000 residents. Split it into municipalities and the uniform color breaks apart: Fukuoka City has 3.23, while Kitakyushu City has 1.42.

Fukuoka City’s ratio is about 2.3 times Kitakyushu City’s. Neither the prefecture average nor one city’s figure describes every place inside its boundary.

Both views use the same 0–5 color scale. Designated-city wards share their parent city’s rate. Select an area for its figures.

10 / Read the map again

People move. Boundaries stay.

Commuters, shopping trips, highway stops, airport journeys: each gives us a reason to look beyond the resident count. A high ratio is a useful clue about a place. To explain it, we need evidence about the people who use that place—and more than one date to measure change.

What the map can tell us

Count the stores. Question the population.

The four examples point to a question worth investigating: how much of a place's activity comes from people who live elsewhere? The stores give us starting points. Commuting data, visitor counts and travel surveys would help test the explanation.

This April snapshot cannot establish which towns are getting richer or which cities will grow. It can show how the answer changes when we choose a different unit of comparison—and why even a familiar measure such as “per person” deserves a second look.

Data & sources

A fixed snapshot, with clear limits.

Stores. The article retains the April 2026 dataset: 2,108 unique store URLs from the Starbucks Japan store locator. It is not a current store inventory. The exact collection day was not recorded. The archived map points, population figures and provenance are available as JSON. The ranking file records a generation date of April 3, 2026. See the prefecture counts and municipality counts.

Population. The denominator is the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2025 Basic Resident Registration: total population, as documented in the population data notes. The bundled prefecture totals sum to 124,330,690. Population and store counts refer to different dates; these are descriptive ratios, not estimates of customer demand.

Calculation and geography. Stores ÷ registered residents × 100,000. The national rate uses the sum of prefecture populations, rather than an average of local rates. Tokyo's special wards remain separate; wards of designated cities are aggregated to the city level. The geographic files contain simplified 2025 administrative boundaries. Store points come from the coordinates in the archived official listings, and municipality assignment follows each store’s address. Facility labels use approximate anchors within the facilities, not surveyed building centers. The map is intended for comparison, not boundary surveying.

Context sources. Chiyoda's 2020 census results establish the distinction between daytime and resident population; those census figures are not used in the ratios. The mall's official directory identifies the Hiezu location, and its access guide provides routes from surrounding cities. Taga’s two archived stores are identified in the official listings for Taga Service Area (upbound) and EXPASA Taga Service Area (downbound), on the Meishin Expressway. The official airport store listing places the Kansai International Airport 1F store in Tajiri and describes its airport setting. The second store is on the terminal’s second floor. Both Terminal 1 locations are retained from the April store list. Facility locations provide context, not measured customer origins or flows.

Revision. Published April 17, 2026; revised October 4, 2026. The revision corrects the population-source description and comparison arithmetic, removes unsupported economic explanations, and adds geographic context, comparisons between total counts and population ratios, and a closer look inside Fukuoka Prefecture. The April store-count dataset is unchanged.
